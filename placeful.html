From Bloom to Placeful

  • Bloom, nenos's previous app, helped users stay connected with friends and family while discovering events in their local communities.
  • A central part of the experience was Discover, which allowed users to browse event recommendations and find things happening nearby.

The Problem

Bloom could help people discover their community, but

gave them few ways to meaningfully participate in it.

Passive discovery

Users could find events, but there was little motivation within the product to actually engage with them.

Limited participation

Beyond discovering events, posting, and messaging, there were few meaningful ways for users to interact with their community.

Disconnected offline impact

Because event participation happened outside the app, nenos had limited visibility into whether discovery translated into real-world engagement.

The Solution

From discovering your community to...

becoming a part of it!

is a completely new social experience that would build on Bloom's community-focused mission while creating a stronger connection between users' digital identities and their real-world experiences.

At the center of is Places:

Places reimagines the traditional social media profile as a customizable 2D space, called a “Place”, representing where a user goes, what they experience, and the communities they participate in.

Users can populate their “Place” by attending events and earning “Mementos”: digital souvenirs that represent their real-world experiences and memories.

Discover → Attend → Verify → Collect a Memento → Build your Place

Our Challenge

Bring Places to life by designing how users would create their Places, collect Mementos, and interact with this new experience.

Divide and Conquer

To tackle the scope of Placeful, we divided the experience into three focus areas:

PROFILE & ONBOARDING

Creating an onboarding experience that sets users up for success after they earn a memento.

SHARING & CUSTOMIZATION

Designing a personalized experience where users can curate their Place.

DISCOVER & COLLECT

Verifies visits, crafts a memento-arrival UX, and frames the experience before, during, and after.

Sub-Problem Statements

  • Verification: How does the system know the user was there across venue types, ranging from a ticketed music festival to a state park with no staff or infrastructure?
  • Timing: When does the “memento” arrive, and what does the arrival feel like?
  • Framing: Where does the “memento” appear in the interface before, during, and after the visit?

Usability Testing

We conducted 6 usability tests:

  • 2 live, moderated sessions
  • 4 asynchronous, interaction tests

Key Insights:

The “Going” button created ambiguity.

Users were unsure whether it meant expressing interest or registering for an event, despite it being required to claim a Memento.

The memento states were not clear enough on their own.

Users sometimes interpreted the blurred memento as a low-quality image, missed the visual change after unlocking it, or were unsure whether and where their memento had been saved.

Users gravitated toward familiar event-browsing behaviors.

Rather than focusing on the new memento experience, most users were attracted to event details, comments, and other content, causing them to miss memento collection actions.

Users relied on the interface rather than written instructions.

Although some instruction was provided to user testers, many skimmed or dismissed instructions and instead explored on their own, causing some to miss the memento flow entirely.

Key Changes:

Changing the memento icon

Usability testing showed that users interpreted the trophy as an achievement rather than an indicator that an event offered a Memento, so I introduced a more distinct and consistent visual cue (nenos logo).

This made location sharing feel like a natural next step rather than a sudden request, while providing access to nenos’s Privacy Policy for users who wanted more transparency before continuing.

Added a permission screen to the location verification flow

Added a delayed Memento notification 1-2 hours after check-in

This lets users stay focused on enjoying the event rather than immediately deciding what to do with their Memento, while creating a natural opportunity to revisit it afterward.

Clearer success states and visual hierarchy confirm when verification is complete and when a Memento has been collected, helping users understand what happened and what to do next.

Strengthened feedback throughout the memento journey.

Redesigned the event and Discover pages.

Reworked both pages to create clearer information hierarchy, make Memento opportunities easier to discover, and integrate collection and registration more naturally into the existing event experience.

Key Takeaways

  1. Designing within an existing product
  • I learned how to balance integrating a new feature into an established experience while also recognizing when the existing experience needed to evolve.
  • Rather than forcing Mementos into screens that weren’t designed for them, I redesigned parts of the surrounding experience when necessary.
  1. Research challenges designer assumptions
  • Usability testing showed me that interactions that felt intuitive to me while designing weren’t always what were intuitive to real users.
  • Several users struggled to even enter the intended flows, reinforcing the importance of observing actual behavior rather than relying on designer assumptions.
  1. Communication is part of the design process
  • Working with a real client and presenting our work weekly taught me how important it is to clearly communicate not only what I designed, but why I made particular decisions and how research supported them.
  1. Consistency requires collaboration
  • With our design team divided across three subteams, maintaining a unified design system became essential.
  • Shared typography, colors, components, iconography, and interaction patterns helped three parallel workstreams to ultimately feel like one product.

Reflection

This project pushed me beyond thinking about design as simply creating the best interface…

I had to learn how to work within an existing product, decide when existing experiences needed to change, communicate my decisions to different stakeholders, and balance exploration with constraint.

One of the most surprising moments came during usability testing. Interactions that had seemed completely intuitive while we were designing them were sometimes overlooked entirely by users—some didn’t even enter the flows we expected them to test. Watching that happen reminded me how easily designers can become too familiar with their own work, and how important it is to let real user behavior challenge our assumptions.

Working on a larger cross-functional team also taught me that good product design rarely happens in isolation. Between coordinating across three design subteams, meeting with our client, collaborating with product managers and developers, and presenting our work to industry professionals, I became much more conscious of how clearly I communicate my reasoning and how my individual decisions contribute to a larger product experience.

Most importantly, I learned that having creative freedom doesn’t mean pursuing every possible idea. It means knowing when to explore, when to redesign, and when to constrain an idea so that every decision ultimately serves the user and the product.
